Each project also carries its original budget (`pipeline/budget_history.py`): the Capital Projects Dashboard's budget history (`qj5n-h5qp`) holds one original-budget row per record, dated when first recorded (2006 on), then the same triannual series from 2023-05, which also fills periods a project is missing from in the snapshots.

7. **Deployment to GitHub Pages.** Done 2026-10-03. The site is static, so no server is needed. The workflow fetches the export from a GitHub Release asset, so data files stay out of the main branch.
8. **New data domains.** This is the current phase. The full picture of what the city and its authorities build comes first, because it decides the story the site tells and the interface that tells it. School Construction Authority projects (`pipeline/sca.py`, `sca_locations.py`, with history from archived versions in `sca_history.py`) and the MTA capital program (`pipeline/mta.py`, `mta_locations.py`, `mta_spending.py`) are built and exported as programs alongside the city's, off by default until the map redesign. The city's original budgets are exported (`pipeline/budget_history.py`). Next: population by district (`pipeline/census.py`) when the story needs it. The schedule model and the spending classification across sources are built (below).
9. **A scrolling story.** One page where text cards scroll over a pinned map, each card a saved map state computed from the data, ending with the full map unlocked. New visitors see it first; shared links and returning visitors go straight to the map. Approach, card rules and candidate cards: `docs/story.md`. Candidate material from CPDB's releases (`cpdb_history_funding`, `cpdb_history_geoms`): funding-split changes per project between releases (a federal share appearing with a grant; how much of the city's capital work leans on federal money over time; planned amounts cover a rolling plan window and releases before 2024 are planned-only, so compare 2024 on), point and footprint moves (projects CPDB re-sited, and how stable its placements are), and when projects enter and leave CPDB beside their city status.
10. **Map and views redesign,** shaped by the data and the story: one mark per place rather than per project, drawing that changes with zoom (shading at city scale, then clusters, then points and outlines), Tiers A and B drawn alike with precision explained in the project panel, and larger touch targets. Then the remaining views (leaderboard, spend progress).
    - **Aggregation by zoom level.** Distinct zoom levels, each aggregating the projects within it into one larger mark: borough, district and neighborhood (the area levels the site has), then a block, then one place (the same point, or points within a small distance). Each mark can show the theme mix, for example as a pie of themes. When the zoom level changes, the projects in a mark animate out to their marks at the next finer level, or in to their mark at the next coarser one.
    - **Point size with zoom.** Points grow as the map zooms in, on a non-linear scale, and each has a hit area larger than its drawn size; the smallest points are now hard to select, and almost impossible on a phone (`docs/ui-plan.md`, Usability findings).

### What the redesign emphasises
The site answers residents' questions about what is being built near them, so the redesign centres on progress:
- **Completion.** Completed projects are hidden by default; they remain in the data and appear in views over time (budget and schedule history, the story).
- **Schedules.** Projects with a reported schedule come first, because only they can say when work will finish. Every source of schedules for projects that lack one is worth pursuing (SCA's phase dates, agency trackers such as the Parks capital tracker, CPDB).
- **Questions near a place:** "what near me is close to completion?" and "what near me has fallen behind schedule or gone over budget?" (signed schedule and budget variance against earlier reports).

### One schedule model across sources
Sources publish schedules differently, so each must be mapped to one model before the redesign can show or rank them together. The city publishes a project-level forecast completion per PID, revised each report; "late" there means the forecast moved, and there is no published baseline. SCA publishes per-phase planned ends, set when a phase starts and almost never revised; "late" means the actual end, or the version date, is past that planned end (`sca_trends.days_late`). The common model, built in the pipeline and exported per project:
- **Phases** under shared names (planning and design, procurement, construction, close-out), each with start, planned or forecast end, actual end and the version it comes from.
- **Summary:** expected finish; baseline finish (the first published, or the first held, labelled as such: the city's earliest forecast from May 2023); days late against the baseline; slip since the previous report; state (not started, under way, finished); and a `schedule_rule` naming how the source's figures were derived.
Every later source (MTA, state) is mapped to the same model. Views then answer "close to completion" and "behind schedule" in one set of units, while the project panel says which rule produced them.

Built (`pipeline/schedules.py`: `project_schedule`, `project_phases`; exported as each project's schedule fields and `schedule_phases.json`). Dates carry a precision (MTA publishes months, sometimes only a year), and differences are taken at the coarser one. The city's finish is the latest of its PIDs', from the schedule history or else the snapshot's forecast completion; `has_schedule` means a dated expected finish. MTA's original completion is a published baseline (it is rarely restated, unlike its original budget); where MTA publishes none, the first completion held is used, labelled so. SCA gives no expected finish once a phase is overdue. "Late" measures something different in each program (forecast moved, past a phase's planned end, past the original completion), so views must name the rule.

### One spending classification across sources
Every source mixes construction with money that is not a project at a place. Each project in every source gets a reviewed `spending_kind`, with evidence from the record's own text:
- **physical work**, located or not (whether it has a place comes from its location tier: DOT's in-house resurfacing crews and MTA's Platform Screen Doors pilot are physical work with no single place). A cost that belongs to one named project (its design, construction management, insurance, real estate or contingency) is that project's physical work, as a city project's budget includes its own design and construction-management fees;
- **overhead** (agency-wide insurance, program administration, independent engineers and monitors, general engineering consultants and program management, small business program administration, enterprise IT systems, studies not about a particular place, and scope development or design for projects not yet defined).

Money set aside (a reserve) is not a kind of its own: it takes the kind of what it is for, and a separate reserve flag keeps visible how much of a plan is not yet assigned to contracts. Agency-wide contingency, if it appears, is held for review. How the work is delivered (in-house crews, a pass-through fund to another public entity such as the Brooklyn Navy Yard or Governors Island trusts, a funding agreement) is a separate field, not a kind. Default views show physical work; overhead stays counted as its own labelled line rather than disappearing.

MTA's ACEPs are classified (`pipeline/mta_spending.csv`, drafted by rule and reviewed): of $122.4B live, $117.2B is physical and $5.2B overhead, and $57.3B is flagged as reserves. City and SCA projects are classified the same way (`pipeline/spending.py`; `city_spending.csv` and `sca_spending.csv`, drafted by rule and reviewed). The city's "Not a discrete project" phases describe how work is delivered (funding agreements, in-house crews, pass-through funds, job order and requirements contracts), recorded as `delivery`; nearly all of it is physical work. Of $160.3B in current city projects, $1.25B is overhead: enterprise IT and money not yet tied to defined work ($1.19B flagged as reserves, mostly DEP's "Agency Proposed Outyear Projects"). Every SCA project is physical, its leases included (project-specific real estate).

### Ongoing: the city's capital data
Work on the core city data continues throughout: placing more projects at higher tiers (the remaining location work below), and reviewing ambiguous sources and inferred links (see "Reviewing ambiguous sources") as new official data appears.

The site reads plain JSON and GeoJSON; filters, totals and search run in the browser in JavaScript (5,600 current projects is small). Footprints and history can load on demand.

### Multi-site projects: per-site budget shares
Some projects have several known sites. Each project gets one location, its most central site, plus `spread_m`, the distance from that site to the farthest one. The `project_sites` table keeps every site, with a share of the budget.
- **Shares:** split equally across sites, and labelled as estimates (`share_method = 'equal'`). Shares sum to the project budget, so non-geographic totals are unchanged.
- **Sources of sites:**
  - CPDB points, Parks tracker and DOT/DEP intersections with several points: 104 projects, $3.75B. The median spread is about 4 km; 71 projects spread over 2 km.
  - Titles naming units in different buildings ("26th, 42nd & 46th Precincts"), which are currently left unplaced.
  - At district level, projects listing several community districts (71 projects, $1.2B), split across those districts.
- **Kept alongside:** the single representative point and `spread_m`, so the map and existing metrics still work.
- **Reported in `docs/profile.md`:**
  - money split, and its share of the total budget, by source and tier
  - sites per project (median and maximum)
  - distance from each site to the project's single point (median and 90th percentile)
  - how district and heatmap totals change when shares replace the single point
- **Known proportions:** the Parks capital tracker (`4hcv-tc5r`) publishes `TotalFunding` for each tracker entry. In 37 FMS IDs with several entries the amounts differ by site (P-6POGC15: four pools, $54k to $220k); in 154 others every entry repeats the project total. The entries' sum is not the FMS budget (median 0.90×, mostly 0.56–1.31× in the 22 current projects), so use them as proportions (`share_method = 'source_proportion'`). These projects also measure how far an equal split is from the real one. Loaded as `loc_parks_tracker.total_funding`. DOT/DEP intersection costs and CPDB amounts repeat per project, so they give no split.
- **Better shares later** (unverified ideas): weight sites by lot or floor area from PLUTO, by line length for street work, or by per-site contract amounts if a contract dataset links contracts to sites.
- **Timing:** with the export step, since it changes what the site reads. The multi-unit title parsing can come earlier.

### Project families
Some programs are split into officially separate projects that belong together: DOT's bridges over Amtrak's 30th Street Branch are one FMS ID per street (West 33rd to 40th: eight current projects, about $630M; an earlier round of small repairs covered West 33rd to 50th). A `project_families` table would group them, detected from shared title patterns ('over Amtrak 30th Street Branch'), consecutive bridge numbers (BINs 2-24501-B to F) and shared budget lines, each family reviewed with evidence, so the map can draw a family as one corridor and rankings can treat it as one program. Families are an overlay: the projects stay separate records.

### Network programs
- Programs such as resurfacing, pedestrian ramps and signal work are funded through program-level FMS IDs, but carried out at many sites.
- Operational datasets show where the work happens, such as DOT in-house resurfacing segments (`ffaf-8mrv`, with WKT geometry).
- These link to a program, not to a project, so they belong on the map as program overlays rather than project pins.

### Linear projects drawn along their stretch
Rail and street work is a corridor, not a point: MTA publishes one `base` point for some corridor projects (IBX project development, G9080101, sits on 56th St at 12th Ave in Borough Park, several blocks from the Bay Ridge Branch), and street projects are drawn as one centerline line or a point. The IBX ACEPs now have their 18 stations as sites (`ibx.py`), so their money is spread along the line; the line itself is not drawn yet. With the redesign's heatmaps and other views, draw such projects along the whole stretch: rail lines from an official rail layer (the city's planimetric railroad lines; the street centerline omits rail), street work from the centerline (`street_lines.py`). A published point well off the stretch it stands for is then measurable and recorded with that evidence.
- **Stretches that don't route:** 28 projects name a stretch between two cross streets that `street_lines.py` cannot route along the centerline. In 18 a cross street never touches the named street: the street continues under another name (Woodhaven Blvd becomes Cross Bay Blvd before 107 Av; Travis Av at Park Dr N) or ends short of it (Worth St and E Broadway). In 9 both cross streets touch but no path joins them (gaps in the centerline, divided roadways: 146 Av, W 30 St, Woodrow Rd), and in 1 a cross street is misread (HWMNYPL's '6' for 6 Av). Only 9 sit below Tier A (2 at D: Woodhaven Blvd SBS Phase B, Travis Av; 7 at B), so a fix (detecting a street's continuation under another name, or snapping to the nearest node within a distance to be defined) waits for drawing stretches. A range that repeats no street ('Broadway from W 102nd to 104th Streets and from W 137th to 139th Streets', P-307BMAP) draws only its first stretch.

### Area served: open work
`pipeline/serving.py` classes each project local, regional, citywide or outside the city. Work to refine it:
- **Official service areas:** regional work counts in its site's borough unless an official service area is published as data. About half the regional money serves several districts rather than a whole borough, so the borough is too broad for it (current projects when measured): transit, about $29.6B, now counted in the districts its stations serve (`pipeline/project_areas.py`: where existing stations' morning riders live, or the residents within 800 m of stations not yet built and of lines without origin-destination data); wastewater treatment plants and CSO trunk sewers, about $18.1B, which need drainage areas; economic development sites, $8.7B; H+H hospitals, $5.1B, which need patient-origin or catchment data; community and large parks, about $3.4B. Only transit has a measured area so far; the rest counts by borough. No DEP wastewater drainage areas were found on NYC Open Data (searched "drainage area", "sewershed", "wastewater treatment plant"); H+H catchments and economic development service areas are not yet searched. Work serving a whole borough (borough-based jails, courts, borough seats, shelters, high schools, highways and bridges) is counted correctly by borough. A record naming several boroughs ("City Tunnel 3 Stage 2 Brooklyn/Queens leg", "Citywide Milling - Brooklyn and Staten Island") could split its money across those boroughs.
- **Usage beyond the subway:** subway station and line work is classed by where its morning riders live (`pipeline/ridership.py`). The same could apply to NYC Ferry landings (NYC Ferry Ridership, `t5n6-gx8c`), the Staten Island Ferry (`6eng-46dm`) and commuter rail stations, if their published counts allow it.
- **Boardings a catchment can produce:** `ridership.py` caps the morning boardings it credits to a station's catchment at the 90th percentile of boardings per resident among "residential" stations (arrivals under half of boardings), about 0.14 a day when set; the rest count as transfers. Both cut-offs are arbitrary. Ground the cap in a measured rate instead, such as the share of residents who commute by subway (Census ACS journey to work, table B08301, by tract) or a model fitted across all stations, and test how sensitive the classes are to it. Riders above the cap are left out of the shares (their homes are unknown). Revisit that at the same time: a split by transfer type (bus feeders from the same borough, commuter rail, PATH and ferry riders from outside it) was tried with hub points from MTA Rail Stations (`wxmd-5cpm`), PATH stations (`bbqn-x8xv`) and FacDB's ferry and bus terminals within 200 m. It misfired: Flushing–Main St's small LIRR station made its bus riders count as from outside Queens, and Times Sq and Penn (A,C,E) stand more than 200 m from the hub points. It would need ridership per hub, or only the major terminals measured from each entrance.
- **Ridership class cut-offs:** station and line work is local when two-thirds of its placeable morning riders live in the stations' own districts, regional when two-thirds live in their borough (`ridership-*` rules in `serving_rules.csv`), and a station's own districts are those holding 10% of its catchment (`AREA_MIN`). Two-thirds is borrowed from the Statement of Needs tally rule, not measured, and the classes depend on it: of 189 current station and line projects, a 50% cut-off makes 145 local, 39 regional and 5 citywide; two-thirds 95, 70 and 24; 75% 48, 92 and 49. Pooled line work is measured the same way: the 8th Avenue line's Manhattan stations have 65% of riders living in Manhattan, just under two-thirds, so its track work is citywide while every other named line is regional. Define these with the boardings cap: a majority is the simplest to explain; a cut-off measured from facilities the Statement already classes would need their usage data.
- **Commuter rail network work:** LIRR and Metro-North work naming no place is split by the railroad's share of stations inside the city (LIRR about 21%, Metro-North 13%), a count, not riders; replace it with ridership by station if MTA publishes it. Define it with the other provisional figures.
- **Places named but not in a station list:** Metro-North's Mott Haven Yard and Melrose Tunnel drainage (M9030105) names a Bronx place but is split as railroad-wide work; yards, shops and tunnels named in titles need an official point (FacDB, GNIS or an MTA record).
- **Unnamed stations:** MTA ACEPs such as "Ada: 23 Stations" and "Station Components: Various Locations" are systemwide with no points, so they count only citywide. A station list from an official MTA record would place them. MTA's points for multi-station ACEPs carry no per-station amounts, so their budget is split equally (`mta_sites`).
- **Placement gaps found in review:** the Schomburg Center and the Library for the Performing Arts sit at Tier D/E, because their FacDB names have words the titles lack (a `named_features.csv` row each would place them). Borough civic buildings named only by address (120-55 Queens Blvd, 851 Grand Concourse, 10 Richmond Terrace) are now classed by the city lot of that address (Geoclient's BBL in COLP), which moved 14 projects from citywide to regional.
- **Commuter rail in the city:** LIRR and Metro-North stations inside the city are classed regional by judgement. Measuring them like the subway needs published ridership per station or per station pair; whether data.ny.gov holds it is not yet checked.
- **Projects partly outside the city:** MTA work with most of its budget share at sites outside the five boroughs is classed outside. A project with only some sites outside (LIRR "Ada: 7 Stations", 14%) keeps its class, so the per-resident counts must drop those sites' shares (`project_serving.outside_share`).
- **Distance falloff for parks and other area-serving sites:** a community or large park serves several districts but benefits those nearest it most. Instead of counting its money evenly over a borough (or its districts), weight districts by distance from the site with a falloff (a normal or sigmoid curve). The curve's shape and width would need defining from measured use (park visitation surveys, if any are published), not chosen by hand.
- **Placeholder districts:** DCAS energy projects (`ACE`, `SOLAR`, `EO26`, and the `SCA..ELE` school electrification lump sums) listing only district 01s are Tier D in a placeholder district; `project_areas.py` counts them by borough, and the lump sums, which name no site, citywide. Their sites need placing from another record.
- **Transit area constants:** regional station work counts within the stations' own boroughs, those holding 10% of the 800 m catchment (`AREA_MIN`, shared with the ridership classes), with Brooklyn and Queens joined because riders near their land border live on either side. Stations by the Harlem River (Marble Hill-225 St, 215 St) keep both boroughs through their catchments. Define the 10% with the other ridership constants. The 800 m radius is the half mile MTA used as each new station's study area in the Penn Station Access Environmental Assessment (Executive Summary p. ES-6); its four stations hold 109,160 residents within 800 m (2020 census, tract parts inside the circle), against the Assessment's "over 130,000" within a half mile (Chapter 1, p. 1-7; its method is not stated). Commuter rail riders also arrive by bus and car from farther out: the Assessment's modal split tables for the station study areas could weight a wider area.

## New data domains

### MTA capital program
- Candidate source: "MTA Capital Dashboard Project Locations" (`wcsa-vkhf` on data.ny.gov), which includes coordinates. Its keys and coverage have not yet been assessed.
- data.ny.gov runs Socrata, so `pipeline/socrata.py` works with a different base URL.
- MTA projects have no FMS ID, so they are a separate layer or view, not merged into city projects.

Assessed (data.ny.gov): the project is the ACEP (`proj_num`), the money record in the Capital Dashboard (`ehz8-ag3n`: 24 quarterly loads from March 2020, original, latest-approved and current budgets and dates, phase, location indicator). All live ACEPs count, whatever their plan (latest load: 1,360 live, $122.4B; the 2010–14 and 2015–19 plans hold $9.0B of it); Complete and Superseded ACEPs stay in the history but out of live totals. C&D's large projects (`9hy6-8j6t`, with schedules `nswv-d6bz` and monthly budgets by ACEP `f6fd-xfps`) and the dashboard's `mega_project` group ACEPs; they are groupings, not a second count. Locations (`wcsa-vkhf`): single points (`base`) are Tier A; multi-location points mark actual work sites (stations, pump rooms), so the budget is split equally across them; line-wide work such as CBTC on various lines is drawn as simply as possible until the redesign decides. Systemwide, rolling-stock and `dollar` ACEPs have no location. Ten points have latitude and longitude swapped and one longitude is mis-scaled. No overlap with the city's capital data is expected.

Allocation change is measured over groups, not per ACEP (`pipeline/mta_growth.py`): each plan's total at every approval date in MTA's funding plans (`6kvv-fcph`, from 2005; `mta_plan_amendments`), split into money on newly listed ACEPs and changes to listed ones, and each mega project's fixed set of ACEPs per dashboard load (`mta_mega_series`, from March 2020). The funding plans dataset does not record every amendment on its date: many ACEPs carry an amendment's change only on the latest row (2026-06-30), whose narrative names the earlier amendment ('reprogrammed per the MTA Board approved July 2013 Plan Amendment'; 1,482 ACEPs change there, -$18.6B in all), and money moved to new ACEPs keeps counting on the old one until then. So totals between adoption and the latest row overstate plans and mega projects, and the 2005–09 plan is mostly first listed on the latest row ($7.0B at adoption, $23.8B there). Not even its first rows are the adopted plans (2010–14: $29.28B against $26.265B adopted; 2015–19: $38.37B against $29.456B; 2020–24: $62.02B against $54.799B); its latest rows come within about 2% of the latest approved totals. Each plan's approvals are taken instead from MTA's amendment documents and board items (`mta_program_approvals`, every figure quoted with its page and checked against the PDF): the 2020–24 plan was adopted at $54.799B in September 2019 and stands at $55.432B after Amendment #5 (December 2025); the 2015–19 plan's first $32.046B version was vetoed by the CPRB in October 2014, it was adopted at $29.456B in 2016 and stands at $33.619B; the 2010–14 plan went from $26.265B to $34.801B with the Sandy amendments and back to $31.561B. Amendments mostly move money between plans and add funding, so these are not cost growth alone. No 2005–09 document was found in MTA's library. Each amendment book's Network Expansion table is transcribed line by line (`mta_mega_amendments`, quoted and checked: each line's change and each table's total add up, and consecutive books agree; one printing error, in 2020–24 Amendment #3, is listed in `data_issues`). Summed over the 2015–19 and 2020–24 plans, the books equal the dashboard's mega project series to $0.1M at both ends: Penn Station Access $1,583.1M then $3,379.2M, Second Avenue Subway Phase II $6,290.0M then $6,968.2M (the dashboard adds the $16.0M 125th Street Subway ACEPs, which the book lists as Second Avenue Subway West, not part of Phase 2), LIRR Expansion $2,588.5M then $2,488.5M. So the dashboard's mega series is sound, and Penn Station Access's rise is real: the adopted 2020–24 plan held $1,131.1M, raised to $2,748.7M by Amendments #1 and #2 (2021–22) and to $2,948.7M by #3 (2023); the funding plans dataset dates that money to its January 2020 rows. The June 2010 book also gives each 2010–14 line's funding in earlier plans: East Side Access $4,374M before 2010 and $2,954M in 2010–14 ($7,328M). Still to extract: each amendment's agency table, the 2015–19 books before Amendment #4, the 2025–29 plan's table, and each mega project's money in earlier plans from later books. C&D's goal and estimated costs (`9hy6-8j6t`) are equal on nearly every project and held in one copy, so they are not a baseline yet; `f6fd-xfps` has a monthly baseline budget and expenditures for 191 large-project ACEPs.

### What a taxpayer paid into which projects (idea)
An estimate of what a New York City or State taxpayer in a given income bracket paid, over a period, into which capital projects. City capital is borrowed (general obligation and Transitional Finance Authority bonds) and repaid through debt service from city taxes; water and sewer work is repaid through water rates, the MTA and SCA are funded differently. A bracket's share of the taxes that pay debt service, times each project's share of debt-financed spending, would give the figure. Candidate sources: the State's personal income tax by income bracket and place of residence (data.ny.gov `73iw-kuxv`, `qjqv-zrwt`, `5bb2-yb85`; unverified), the Comptroller's Annual Report on Capital Debt and Obligations, and each project's spending by fiscal year (`gyhf-rsr3`). It is a model, not a measurement, so it would carry its assumptions with it (which taxes fund debt service, property tax incidence, timing between spending and repayment).

## Reviewing ambiguous sources
Some links between records rest on inference rather than a shared key, and are reviewed again as new official data appears:
- **SCA buildings placed by inference** (`pipeline/sca_sites.csv`, Tier B): the Medgar Evers College Prep annex, the Bronx STEAM Center and Life Academy High School for Film and Music. A DOB filing or DOE list that names their building code would make them exact.
- **SCA name matches** (`sca_buildings` sources `facdb_name` and `facdb_number`): many are annexes, where the match lands on the main building.
- **City money in SCA projects** (`pipeline/sca_city_links.csv`): 14 `possible` links, where DCAS energy FMS IDs (mostly FY24, since dropped from the city data; K005 is the only current one) name a school at which SCA reports similar work it does not label DCAS or ACE. The city amounts are mostly smaller than SCA's, so the city record may fund only part of the work (K420 is the exception: city $17.5M, SCA $5.6M); both are counted. Until official records settle them, the site should at least report the ambiguity: on the project panel of both records, and in any total that includes both. Four DCAS lump sums for FY28–30 name no building yet. SCA also labels 86 small projects ACE (DCAS's energy program); only three match a city record that names their building (`ACEDOE211`), so the rest may be funded through city records that name no school.
- **Project Connect's program figures** (`pipeline/sca_repeats.csv`): whether the 2009 and 2019 amounts overlap, and which schools they cover. Every earlier version (2021 to March 2026) shows the same schools with small estimates ($14.0M and $1.1M in total) that in August 2026 moved to the spending column while the program amount filled every row's estimate. This reading is an assumption until the next SCA update confirms it; a data check fails once a newer version arrives.
- **New sources:** SCA's site-selection notices in the City Record (`dg92-zbpx`) give block and lot for new school sites but not SCA's building code.
- **Forecasts that are not a project's finish** (`pipeline/schedule_reviews.csv`): the city's forecast completion sometimes marks a milestone. Newtown Creek's CSO tunnel (`CS-NC-CST`, PID 634) forecast 2029 while the state's CSO consent order (DEC Case No. CO2-20000107-8, 2024 modification) sets design completion in December 2028, construction notice to proceed in December 2029 and construction completion in December 2040; its forecasts are left out and the order's finish carried. Other large projects whose forecast falls near a design or procurement milestone may have the same problem; check them against their official schedules as they are found.
- **MTA funding plans' latest date (`6kvv-fcph`, 2026-06-30):** it appears in every plan, and each plan's total there equals its current budget in the March 2026 dashboard load; 733 2005–09 ACEPs are first listed on it. It may be the current state rather than a Capital Program Review Board approval, which changes what its step means (−$6.0B for 2010–14, +$9.9B for 2005–09, mostly newly listed ACEPs). `mta_plan_amendments.note` marks those rows. Check against MTA's CPRB submissions and board materials.
- **Unclear source errors:** the `unclear` rows in `pipeline/source_errors.csv` (15 when written).
- **Source rows for city placements:** `project_locations` records the source, the matched name and flags, but not which CPDB row, Parks tracker entry or DOT/DEP intersection a point came from. Under the provenance rule it should; add it when `locations.py` is next changed.
- **Tier B precision is measured in-sample** (against projects that also have a Tier A point), so it likely overstates precision on the projects Tier B actually places, as `docs/profile.md` notes.
- **SCA and DOE points for the same building** differ by a median of 59 m and up to 1 km, on the same address. SCA's points equal Geoclient's address point; DOE's may sit on the building. 564 buildings use DOE's point because SCA lists none.
- **Placeholder community boards** (DCAS energy programs; see "Unresolved placements") make board-based checks and Tier D placements unreliable for those programs.
- **Original budgets:** the change from a project's original budget to its current one is a change in FMS commitments over up to 20 years (added phases and newly registered contracts as well as overruns), not cost growth alone. How the site presents it is open. DPR's rows without spend that differ from the period's published budget (often 1.3 times it) are recorded in `budget_history_issues`; what they measure is unknown.
- **CPDB history:** CPDB (`fi59-268w` funding, `h2ic-zdws` points, `9jkp-n57r` polygons) is used in its latest state. Past versions come from Internet Archive captures of the official CSV and JSON exports (`pipeline/fetch_cpdb_archive.py`, digest-checked, indexed in `data/raw/cpdb/archive/index.json`); where both formats hold a version they agree row for row. Every row names its version (`ccpversion`, `fisa_2022` to `fisa_2026`), but one label can cover several releases (the dataset's `rowsUpdatedAt` tells them apart), so versions are identified by content. Releases held: funding October 2022, December 2022, July 2023, September 2024, January 2025, June 2025 and December 2025; points and polygons October 2022, July 2023, August 2024, January 2025, February 2025 (geometry corrected, money unchanged) and December 2025, and polygons also December 2022. Column names differ by era (2022–23 JSON point and polygon files use shapefile-truncated names such as `descriptio`; 2024–26 point and polygon CSVs use abbreviations such as `magenacro`, `pctotal`). Projects drop out of CPDB, so older releases place projects the current one doesn't: 551 of our projects have a CPDB point or polygon only in an older release (286 of them now Tier C, D, E or unplaced), and 1,027 have a funding split only in an older release. `pipeline/cpdb_history.py` holds every release (`cpdb_history_funding`, `cpdb_history_geoms`). Older releases now place projects the current one has no geometry for: lowest in Tier A for projects CPDB no longer lists (254 when set), Tier B for those it still lists without geometry (135), since dropping the geometry may have been a correction. The latest older release that splits a project's non-city money gives the split when the current one doesn't: 216 projects when set, 58 absent from the current release, 81 listed there with no money and 77 listed as city money only while FMS reports non-city money ( `split_release`, `split_basis`). Measures of change between releases are listed with the story (step 9).
- **SCA delay reasons (parked):** SCA's quarterly reports to the Council (Local Law 24 of 2003) give a written explanation for each late project, which the Open Data table lacks; their schedule variance is already computable from the table (`sca_trends.days_late`). Only the June 2017 edition is in Legistar, and the Internet Archive holds PDFs from 2011–2016. Next step: search the Government Publications Portal (a860-gpp.nyc.gov, which blocks automated access) for recent editions of the "Quarterly Schedule and Budget Report". If 2024–2026 editions are there, extract the explanations for overdue projects, checking each edition against the Open Data figures; otherwise leave it.
- **SCA history:** SCA publishes only its current state. Our own dated copies in `data/raw/sca/` start in August 2026. Earlier versions of the official export (`2xh6-psuq` CSV) come from Internet Archive captures (`pipeline/fetch_sca_archive.py`): each is checked against the archive's SHA-1 digest and recorded with its capture time and URL in `data/raw/sca/archive/index.json`. `pipeline/sca_history.py` builds `sca_history` from them. Usable versions: October 2015 (spending written as `$39600.00`), March 2021, December 2022, August 2023, June 2024, September 2024, December 2024 (re-captured unchanged through February 2025) and March 2026. No current project goes back to 2015, so that version adds history only for projects SCA has since dropped. The May 2014 capture has the current header over rows in another layout and is unusable. Older SCA projects marked complete drop out of the source, so each version also holds projects no later one has. The dataset has the same schedule and cost fields as the quarterly reports SCA sends the Council under Local Law 24 of 2003; those PDFs add schedule variance and delay reasons, but only the June 2017 edition was found in an official archive (Legistar).

## Site content and presentation
- **Glossary:** every agency and program acronym (DDC, DEP, DOT, DCAS, EDC, HHC, SCA, ACEP, FMS, CPDB...) spelled out on first use and in a glossary page.
- **Display names:** readers' forms in presented text ("The Bronx"), data forms ("Bronx") in the data.
- **Methods changelog:** a public page listing each change that moves published numbers (a new source, a reclassification, a location rule), with its date and the data release it first appears in; each data publish also gets a git tag.
- **Themes rework:** the theme scheme is redesigned, through several design rounds, before the story shows it. Theme colours are redone with it: with SCA and MTA projects shown, Transportation's orange (MTA) and the slate shared by the smallest themes (which include Education, so every SCA project) dominate the map.
- **Interactive charts:** Observable Plot for charts and D3 for scrubbers, transitions and custom pieces; prototyped as review pages, then built into the site. Interaction is used where it adds something (time scrubbing, timelapses of change, hover details, linked map and chart).
- **Spending classification recheck:** projects classified physical because a facility's address is in the title may be overhead (studies, administration) at that address; recheck before a spending-kind filter is offered.
- **Precision of official points:** even Tier A points can stand for an office or a centroid rather than where the work happens; measure how often, using the CPDB release history and the golden set.

## Pipeline speed
- **`pipeline/export.py` takes about 3–5 minutes.** Not yet profiled; the likely cost is the pure-Python point-in-polygon lookups (`geo.py`) that assign every site and project to a community district and NTA. Profile it first; candidate fixes are computing district and NTA once per site in an earlier step (stored with the site) or doing the lookup in DuckDB.
